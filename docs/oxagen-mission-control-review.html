The scale-back question

An investor read the Mission Control spec and told you to build less. He is reading a real signal and prescribing the wrong fix. The problem is not that ten pages is too many. The problem is that all ten are load-bearing, so nothing in the product answers the question a buyer asks first: what do I turn off the day I sign?

The call, before the argument

Cut The ontology engine, Neo4j, and the in-app agent

Three connectors, entity resolution, embeddings, a Cypher layer, a second datastore with a capacity ceiling you flagged yourself, and a chat assistant that sells nothing. Roughly eight weeks of the twenty-four, and one whole class of on-call.

Keep Fleet, Run, Spend, and the toolbelt

The flight recorder, the money, and the reason security signs off. These work on day one with no configuration, which is the only thing that matters when the install is 60 seconds.

Move Witness to the front

It is scheduled last, at weeks 18 to 24. It is the most defensible idea in either document and the only one that makes an outside party depend on your records.

The investor

Right about the symptom, wrong about the cure

Where he is right

He cannot repeat your pitch back to you. Ten pages, 96 tools, three stores, 35 tables, seven oracle kinds, and a 24-week milestone plan describe a category, not a purchase. Nothing in that list tells him which line item on a customer's budget dies.

He is also right that the roadmap spends its first five months on things that do not close a deal. Weeks 13 to 20 go to the ontology engine and the audit archive. The proof layer, which is the actual moat, starts in week 18.

And the pricing has moved three times in about a month: the governed action in ADR-052, then per run with volume tiers in this spec, then back to the governed action with a $250k ceiling. Each move rewrites the meter, the invoice, the Spend page, and the sales call. That reads as pre-product more than any page count does.

Where he is wrong

"Scale it back" usually means build less. Build less and you ship a feature, and a feature in this category is a settings page the model providers add for free next quarter. Datadog, Vanta, and Okta all carry more surface than ten pages. Surface is not the liability.

The liability is that the ten pages are presented as ten equal things. A control plane with a clear order of operations reads as a system. The same control plane with no order reads as unfinished.

He is also discounting the strongest fact you have. One engineer, eight months, a live platform, three paying design partners, and a customer council of nine is an outlier, not a red flag. It is only a red flag when the spec is the artifact doing the talking.

Business mechanics

The meter does not measure the value

The product surface is defensible. The commercial model underneath it is where I would spend the next argument, because four things in it work against each other.

You price the recording, not the finding

Runs are the meter at $0.30, $0.20, and $0.12. The value you describe is findings ranked by money at stake and proven spend against unproven spend. A customer who saves $40,000 in token waste pays you the same as one who ignores every finding. Nothing in the price follows the outcome.

The fix is already writtenThe Witness spec bills on stamps and releases funds when an outcome verifies. That is the model Mission Control should adopt. Charge for a proven run, report runs and governed actions as secondary meters, and the three-way pricing wobble resolves into one sentence: you pay when the work is verified.

The free tier may be the whole product for your best-fit buyer

Every organization gets 1,000 runs a month free with every governance feature on. Your reference customer runs 5,000 a month and pays about $1,200. A team of ten developers on Claude Code will sit under 1,000 runs for a long time, and that team is exactly who installs in 60 seconds and tells their friends.

FixKeep the free tier generous on volume and limit it on retention and seats instead. Thirty days of evidence and three seats free costs you almost nothing and makes the upgrade a governance decision rather than a volume accident.

The margin claim does not hold at the top tier

The spec states gross margin above 80 percent at every tier, with direct cost of one to four cents per run. At $0.12 a run and four cents of cost, that is 67 percent. The claim only holds if unit cost falls toward one or two cents as volume rises, which is plausible but is not stated.

FixEither state the volume assumption beside the claim or drop the top tier to three tiers ending at $0.15. An investor who checks this arithmetic and finds it loose will discount every other number in the deck.

The enterprise floor has moved from $250k to $60k to none

The spec sets enterprise from $60,000 a year. Your September position had a $250,000 annual minimum, then dropped the minimum entirely. Three numbers for the same line in about a month, and each one implies a different buyer, a different sales motion, and a different hiring plan for Akash.

FixPick the one your three paying partners will actually sign and hold it for two quarters. A wrong price you hold is recoverable. A moving price is the thing that makes a founder look pre-product, and it is cheaper to fix than any line of code on this page.

The fork

You have written two companies

These two documents do not describe one product with a feature in it. They describe two businesses with different buyers, different meters, and different roadmaps. Building both is the surface-area problem the investor is pointing at, and he has probably not even seen the second one.

Witness

The same idea generalized: 14 oracle classes, a signed certificate ledger, and a verify endpoint that lets an auditor or an insurer recompute a stamp from evidence alone and compare bytes.

Buyer
Anyone who must show an agent's work was correct
Meter
Stamps, with funds released on verify
Proof
14 classes, judgment kept out of the path
Build
8 weeks, 5 gated phases

My read is that Witness is the bigger company and the harder one to copy, for one reason that has nothing to do with the oracles. POST /stamps/{id}/verify makes a third party a user of your system. An auditor, an insurer, or your customer's customer recomputes a certificate and gets matching bytes. Nothing else in either document creates an outside dependency on Oxagen's records, and outside dependencies are what stop a platform from being replaced.

The catch is that Witness on its own has no distribution and no recorder. It needs traces, and Mission Control is what captures them. So they are not alternatives. Mission Control is how Witness gets fed, and Witness is why Mission Control is worth more than a dashboard. State that relationship out loud, in one sentence, and the surface area stops looking like scope and starts looking like sequence.

Ninety days

What I would actually do

Weeks 1 to 4

Ship the recorder and the money, and nothing else. Fleet, Run, Spend, and the findings job on top of the gateway you already have. No ontology, no Neo4j, no assistant panel. The install stays at 60 seconds and the first screen a new customer sees has their own numbers on it.

Weeks 3 to 6

Get one before-and-after number from one design partner. Token spend per merged pull request, before Oxagen and after acting on the findings. One customer, one workload, scoped exactly that way. That number replaces about forty slides.

Weeks 5 to 12

Build Witness on its own eight-week plan, against the partner's real requirements. Phase one is twenty of their requirements expressed as records that pass lint, with the partner confirming each one says what they meant. That conversation is also the best qualification call you will ever run.

Week 12

Set one price and hold it. Charge for proven runs. Report runs, governed actions, and retained storage as secondary meters so you can reprice later without rewriting the meter. Then stop touching it.

Throughout

Move the other seven surfaces into an appendix titled "already built." Not deleted, not deprecated, not on the roadmap. Built, working, and not what you are selling this quarter. That single reframe answers the investor without giving up a line of code.

Before you act on any of this

Three questions for Wednesday

The council of nine meets at 4:30. My whole argument rests on one assumption that you can test in an hour, and if the assumption is wrong the ranking above changes.

What did the three paying partners actually buy?

I have ranked Spend first because the pain is budgeted, urgent, and needs no configuration. If all three bought for governance, or for the recorder alone, then Spend-first is my theory and not the market's, and the ranking should follow their money instead of my reasoning.

Has any of them asked for the ontology?

Cutting it is the largest recommendation on this page. It is right if nobody has asked. It is wrong if one of them is already loading Linear and treats the graph as the reason they stay. Ask directly, and discount any answer that sounds like enthusiasm rather than a scheduled use.

Would they pay for a stamp?

Describe the certificate, the verify endpoint, and the idea that an invoice line links to evidence an outsider can recompute. If two of nine lean in, Witness moves to the front of the build and the pricing question answers itself. If none do, keep Witness where the spec has it and I am wrong about the sequence.

In one paragraph

You do not have a surface-area problem. You have an ordering problem that looks like one. The spec already cut 70 pages to ten and 229 contracts to 96, which is more discipline than most seed companies show, and the three surfaces I would cut on top of that (the ontology engine, its datastore, and the in-app agent) I would cut because they cost weeks and close nothing, not because they make you look big.

Do not scale back to look like a seed company. Sequence so the first thing a customer touches is the thing they can measure, and let the rest sit behind the words "already built." That is a stronger position than the investor is imagining, and it is the one you are actually in.
